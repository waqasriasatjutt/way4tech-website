Most implementation checklists are prose with bullet points in front of it. This one is meant to be worked. Print it, put a name against each line, and stop the project moving to the next section until the current one is ticked.

It follows the six phases we run: discovery, solution design, configuration and prototype, data migration, training and cutover, then hypercare. The full engagement structure sits on the [implementation service page](/services/odoo-implementation/), and the calendar it maps onto is in [the timeline, week by week](/blog/odoo-implementation-timeline-week-by-week/).

## Before you sign anything

- [ ] A named decision maker per function, with authority to change a process rather than just describe it
- [ ] A project owner on your side who can free those people up when the schedule needs them
- [ ] A go-live date chosen against your operating calendar, avoiding your busiest weeks and your period close
- [ ] Scope written as outcomes and gaps, not as a list of module names
- [ ] Every gap carries a disposition and a cost: configure, customise, change the process, or leave out
- [ ] A written change-request route for anything raised after sign-off
- [ ] Out of scope stated in writing, including hardware, subscription fees and third-party licences
- [ ] Agreement on which reports are conditions of go-live and which are phase two

## Discovery: what you have to supply

- [ ] An export of the legacy data, plus someone who can explain what the odd columns mean
- [ ] The chart of accounts and the opening balances, signed off by whoever closes your books
- [ ] Sample documents you want back out of the system: a quotation, an invoice, a purchase order, a delivery note
- [ ] Credentials and a technical contact for every system that has to stay connected
- [ ] Real user time for testing and training, blocked out in calendars in advance
- [ ] The reporting pack the business is actually run on, including the spreadsheets

## Discovery: what has to come out of it

- [ ] A walkthrough per function in scope: sales, purchasing, warehouse, finance, HR
- [ ] The exceptions recorded, not just the happy path
- [ ] Every report someone actually reads, listed, with the name of who reads it
- [ ] The numbers the business is managed by identified, because those decide the chart of accounts and the analytic structure
- [ ] The physical operation walked, where there is one: the warehouse, the shop floor, the counter
- [ ] The sceptic interviewed. The person who thinks the project is a mistake usually knows something the sponsor does not
- [ ] A written process map, a numbered gap list, and the module list that follows from it

## Solution design: the decisions to close

- [ ] Big bang or phased, decided on the size of the business rather than on preference
- [ ] How much history to bring. Masters and open items are not optional; full transactional history is a cost decision
- [ ] Chart of accounts, numbering conventions and product coding scheme agreed, in that order
- [ ] User roles and record-level access defined before any user is created
- [ ] Integration boundary fixed: which system is master for customers, for stock, for prices
- [ ] Owner named for the product file and the customer file after go-live
- [ ] Each customisation defended on its merits rather than inherited from the old system
- [ ] A signed scope where every gap has a disposition and a cost

## Configuration and prototype

- [ ] Company, warehouse, journal and pricing structure built
- [ ] A representative slice of masters loaded: your awkward products, not your simple ones
- [ ] Pilot transactions run end to end, from order through to the ledger entry
- [ ] A weekly demo against the process map, with the gap list as the checklist
- [ ] Custom work started only after the standard flow has been run by real users
- [ ] Anything that loops over records or computes across a table checked at real volume, not on demo data
- [ ] Access rights written with each custom model, not added at the end
- [ ] A defect and change log that both sides can see

## Data migration

- [ ] Cleaning and de-duplication done in the extract, before loading, never after
- [ ] Every legacy field mapped to a target field in writing, including the ones you decide to drop
- [ ] Load run in dependency order: partners, products, then balances, then open documents
- [ ] Control totals reconciled against the old system and signed off with whoever closes your books
- [ ] A cut-off date agreed, and changes frozen in the legacy system from that point
- [ ] A repeatable import that logs its errors, so a re-run is not a manual rebuild
- [ ] Duplicate customers caught at creation rather than merged later

## Local compliance, before the build not after it

- [ ] Your e-invoicing model confirmed: clearance, reporting, post-audit, or nothing in force
- [ ] If reporting: the accredited intermediary chosen, contracted, and given its own timeline
- [ ] Every in-scope document type on the test plan, including credit notes, corrections and foreign currency
- [ ] Master data profiled against the validation rules before the build, not after the first rejection
- [ ] A named role with a daily routine for the rejection queue
- [ ] Inbound supplier e-invoices in scope or explicitly out, because receiving is the half people forget
- [ ] Archive designed to be portable and readable independently of the live database
- [ ] Payroll confirmed as localized in the box for your market, or budgeted as a build
- [ ] Requirements attributed to the adviser who confirmed them, in writing. Not to a software vendor

The per-market detail behind those lines is on the country pages, for example [Saudi Arabia](/countries/saudi-arabia/e-invoicing/), [UAE](/countries/uae/e-invoicing/) and [India](/countries/india/e-invoicing/), and the model distinction is worked through in [clearance or reporting](/blog/odoo-e-invoicing-clearance-vs-reporting/).

## Integrations

- [ ] One direction per object, or two-way stated explicitly and deliberately
- [ ] Master of record named per field, not per object
- [ ] Matching key between systems decided, and the behaviour when it is missing
- [ ] Separate test and live credentials, with a staging instance that cannot touch live data
- [ ] Rate limits and pagination read and designed inside, not discovered in production
- [ ] Idempotency on every inbound message, so a replay cannot double-post
- [ ] Failures go to a queue with an owner and an alert, not to a log file
- [ ] Refunds, cancellations, partial shipments and returns on the test plan explicitly
- [ ] A scheduled reconciliation comparing counts and totals on both sides

## Training and UAT

- [ ] Training on your configured system with your data, not a generic instance
- [ ] Short sessions per role, close to the work, rather than one long session for everybody
- [ ] Returns, corrections and cancellations taught deliberately
- [ ] Testing scripted, and run by the people who will use the system daily
- [ ] The sceptics included in the test group
- [ ] A short practical assessment per role, because attendance is not competence
- [ ] Defects triaged into blockers, fix-before-go-live, and phase two
- [ ] Material handed over in editable form with your internal owner named
- [ ] A refresher session scheduled for after the first period close

## Cutover

- [ ] A written runbook with timings, owners and a marked rollback point
- [ ] A full backup taken before anything starts, from a backup routine that has been restored at least once
- [ ] Smoke test of the critical paths before users are let in
- [ ] The legacy system readable but not writable for a defined period
- [ ] Someone present on the floor on the first busy day, for a counter or warehouse operation
- [ ] Redirects mapped, tested on staging and verified again straight after launch, for any web replatform

## Go or no-go

Call it against these, not against the calendar.

- [ ] UAT signed off by each functional area
- [ ] Control totals reconciled and signed
- [ ] No open blockers, and the fix-before-go-live list closed
- [ ] Payroll, where in scope: two consecutive periods reconciling with no unexplained difference
- [ ] Bank output tested with the bank, not only against its specification
- [ ] Every integration retested after the final configuration freeze
- [ ] The rollback point, the freeze time and the person who calls it are all written down
- [ ] The date is not on a period close or in a peak trading week

## The first thirty days

- [ ] Daily triage, tapering as volume settles
- [ ] Reports people actually opened tuned, and the ones nobody opened retired
- [ ] Administrator handover covering users, access, backups and scheduled actions
- [ ] Scheduled actions monitored on outcome, not just on whether the server is up
- [ ] A restore drilled and its duration recorded, because that duration is your real recovery time
- [ ] A written phase two backlog built from everything deferred during UAT
- [ ] A decision on what happens when hypercare ends: a support agreement, or your team

## Who owns what

| Item | Owner |
|---|---|
| Every gap disposition and its cost | Your decision maker for that function |
| The legacy extract and what its columns mean | Your side, named person |
| Mapping, load and reconciliation | Implementation team |
| Chart of accounts sign-off | Whoever closes your books |
| The rejection or exception queue | A named role on your side, daily |
| Product file and customer file after go-live | Named at design time, not after |
| Backups, restores and scheduled actions | Your administrator, or a [support agreement](/services/odoo-support/) |

## FAQs

**Can we use this checklist with another partner?**
Yes. Nothing on it is specific to us. If a vendor cannot tell you who owns each line, that is the finding.

**What if we cannot tick something before the phase ends?**
Then the project is not in the next phase. Moving on with an open item does not save the time, it moves the cost to a worse week.

**Which lines get skipped most often?**
The reconciliation sign-off, the assessment after training, and the restore drill. All three are dull, all three are the ones that hurt.

**We are already live and skipped most of this. Where do we start?**
Backups restored, scheduled actions monitored, and an owner named for every exception queue. Those three stop the bleeding. After that, a read-only [health audit](/services/odoo-audit/) will tell you what else is open, with a severity and an effort against each finding so it can be budgeted rather than filed.

**How long should the discovery section take?**
Long enough to produce a numbered gap list with a cost against every line. On a single-module project that is days. On a multi-country programme it is the phase that decides whether the rest of the plan is real.
