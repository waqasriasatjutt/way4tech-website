Construction Cloud follows the way a contractor already works. If you know these ten ideas, every screen makes sense. If you are new to construction, this chapter is also a short course in how a contractor gets paid.

1. The project (the job)

Everything belongs to a project. One project is one job: one building, one road, one fit-out. Every diary, requisition, inspection, valuation and cost carries the project it belongs to.

The project holds:

  • the client and the contract value,
  • the project manager and the project team (the people who work on it),
  • the site address and its position on the map (used for the site check-in),
  • the bill of quantities, phases and programme,
  • live totals: budget, committed, actual cost, margin and progress.

Open projects from Contracts & Setup › Projects.

Why it matters

Because every record names its project, the project's figures add themselves up. Nobody types a total into a spreadsheet at month end.

2. The bill of quantities (BoQ)

The bill of quantities lists every piece of work in the job, with a quantity, a unit and a rate. For example: "Blockwork, 140 mm, 1,250 m2 at 38.50". Lines are grouped into sections (Substructure, Frame, Finishes and so on).

Each bill line has two sides:

  • Value: quantity times the rate the client pays. This is what you will be paid.
  • Budget: what the work is expected to cost you. This is what you plan to spend.

The bill is the spine of the job. Measurements, valuations, budgets, requisitions, work orders and cost reports all point back to a bill line.

3. Cost and value are different things

People mix these up all the time.

Value Cost
What it is What the client owes you for the work What the work costs you
Where it comes from Bill rates, measured quantities, agreed variations Materials, labour, plant, subcontractors, expenses
Who mostly sees it Commercial, project managers, directors Project managers, buyers, finance, directors

Margin is value minus cost. A job can look busy and still lose money if cost grows faster than value.

Note

Site roles never see costs, rates, wages or margin. The server does not even send those figures to their screen. This is deliberate.

4. Committed and actual

Cost has two stages.

  • Committed: money you have promised but not yet spent. An approved requisition, a confirmed purchase order, a subcontract order, booked plant hire.
  • Actual: money already spent or owed. Materials issued to the work, labour hours costed, subcontract work certified, plant charged, approved site expenses, supplier bills posted.

Why it matters

Waiting for supplier bills to see cost is too late. Committed cost shows the overspend while you can still do something about it.

5. The money chain: from work done to money in the bank

This is how a contractor is paid every month. Each box is a record in the system, and each one is made from the one before it.

  1. Measurement. The surveyor measures the work done against the bill lines and records it in the Measurement Book.
  2. Valuation. Once a month, a valuation adds up the measured work, agreed variations, dayworks, materials on site and preliminaries, all to date.
  3. Application for payment. The valuation becomes an application to the client: "this is what we claim".
  4. Payment certificate. The client's engineer or quantity surveyor certifies what they agree. The certificate shows claimed against certified, line by line, and takes off retention and any advance recovery.
  5. Invoice. Finance raises the client invoice from the issued certificate.
  6. Payment. When the payment is recorded, the certificate is marked paid.

Example

On Harlow Court, valuation 6 values the work to date. The application claims it, the certificate certifies slightly less with a reason for each difference, 5 percent is held as retention, and the invoice is raised for the net amount payable.

Everything is cumulative. Each certificate shows the total to date, then takes off what was certified before, so a mistake last month is corrected automatically this month.

6. Retention

Retention is money the client holds back from each certificate, usually 3 to 5 percent, up to a limit. It is the client's security until the work is finished and the defects are fixed.

  • Half (the first moiety) is usually released at practical completion (handover).
  • The rest (the final moiety) is released at the end of the defects period, often 12 months later.

The Retention Ledger (Commercial & Billing › Retention › Retention Ledger) records every amount held and every release with its due date. You do the same with your subcontractors: you hold retention from them.

Why it matters

On many jobs retention is most of the profit. Companies lose it because nobody chases it. The system chases it for you.

7. Variations

A variation is a change to the work the client asked for: more, less or different. It changes the contract value.

  1. A site instruction records what the client's side asked for, even if it was spoken.
  2. A variation prices the change and is submitted to the client.
  3. Once agreed, it adds to the revised contract value and can be included in the next valuation.

Revised contract value = original contract value + agreed variations.

Watch out

An instruction that is never written down is never paid. Record verbal instructions the same day, even without a price.

8. Subcontractors work the same way, in reverse

You pay subcontractors the way the client pays you: a package is let as a subcontract order, their work is valued each month in a subcontract valuation, you certify it, hold retention, deduct contra charges (costs you paid on their behalf), and their bill is paid.

9. Approvals

Important documents need a signature before they move on: requisitions, purchase awards, subcontract orders, variations, petty cash, site expenses and payment certificates. Who signs depends on the amount. A site manager may approve up to 5,000, a project manager up to 50,000, and a director above that.

Anything waiting for you appears as approvals waiting on you on Home and in Commercial & Billing › Approvals › Waiting on Me.

Note

The person who raised a document can never approve it. This is called segregation of duties.
